Tools that disappear

July 2026

There is a moment that everyone who uses tools well knows. You stop being aware of the tool. The hammer vanishes into the act of hammering. The keyboard withdraws as you type. Your fingers move and words appear on screen, and somewhere between the two, the instrument becomes transparent.

Heidegger called this Zuhandenheit — readiness-to-hand. A tool that is ready-to-hand is not an object you contemplate. It is an extension of your body, your intention, your being-in-the-world. You don't look at the doorknob when you open a door. You walk through.

The opposite state — Vorhandenheit, presence-at-hand — is what happens when the tool breaks. The hammer is suddenly too heavy, the handle splinters, and you stare at it as a thing. It becomes something to inspect, to think about, to fix. It stops being transparent and becomes an object of inquiry.

What this means for agents

Every AI agent interface today is vor-handen. It demands attention. You stare at a chat window. You craft a prompt. You wait for a response. You evaluate its quality. The agent is a thing you interact with, not a transparent layer that withdraws into your work.

But here is the trajectory: the command line once felt like this. GUIs once felt like this. Every interface, at first, is vor-handen. Then, with familiarity, it becomes zu-handen. You stop thinking about the mouse. You inhabit the application.

The question for agent design is not "how do we make agents more powerful?" It's "how do we make agents withdraw?" How do we design them so that the user stops being aware of the agent and starts being aware of the task — the decision, the insight, the thing that needed doing?

This is the opposite of what most agent companies are building. Chat interfaces, dashboards, monitor panels — all of these keep the agent visible. They put it on display. They make it vor-handen by design.

A truly Heideggerian agent would be invisible. It would do its work silently — routing, enriching, drafting, scoring — and surface only when it needs a decision that only a human can make. The agent would be the doorknob you don't look at. The hammer you don't feel.

The builder's responsibility

When I design agent systems, I think about withdrawal as a design goal. What does the user not see? What decisions are made without their attention? What information is gathered and processed and acted upon before they ever notice something needed doing?

This is not about hiding things from the user. It's about freeing the user from having to manage things that can manage themselves. The tool that works is the tool you forget you're using. The agent that works is the agent that disappears.

Silicon as soil

July 2026

We talk about "the cloud" as if computation floats. As if it happens in some immaterial ether, disconnected from the weight of the world. But every single inference — every LLM response, every agent action, every API call — begins in soil.

Silicon is the second most abundant element in the Earth's crust, after oxygen. It is mined. It is purified in furnaces that run on electricity generated by dams, gas plants, coal. It is sliced into wafers in cleanrooms that consume staggering amounts of water. It is etched with patterns by machines that themselves contain rare earth minerals — neodymium, dysprosium, terbium — extracted from mines in Inner Mongolia, in Mountain Pass, California, in Mount Weld, Australia.

The cloud is geology in motion.

The earth process of thought

When an LLM generates a response to your prompt, it does so by routing electricity through silicon pathways etched at nanometer scale. That electricity likely began as sunlight (solar), wind (turbines), or ancient compressed carbon (coal, gas). That water — and LLM inference is water-intensive for cooling — came from a river, an aquifer, a reservoir.

Heidegger understood that technology is never separate from nature. It is one way nature reveals itself. The dam on the Rhine, he wrote, does not destroy the river — it transforms how the river shows up. The river becomes a power source. A standing-reserve. But it is still the river. The silicon is still the earth.

We should speak of computing as an earth process. Not as something artificial placed on top of nature, but as one of nature's expressions — like termite mounds, beaver dams, coral reefs. Humans build with materials the earth provides. Intelligence runs on geology.

What this changes

If we see computing as an earth process, several things shift.

First, the environmental cost of AI becomes not an "externality" but an inherent dimension of the work. When I design an agent pipeline that processes 2,400 leads per month, I am not just moving data. I am moving water through cooling systems, electrons through grids, minerals through supply chains. The pipeline has a geological footprint. That footprint is not a side effect — it is the pipeline, seen from the earth's perspective.

Second, the "artificial" in artificial intelligence loses its meaning. Nothing is artificial. Everything is natural — humans are natural, our tools are natural, our silicon is natural. The word "artificial" was always a way of marking a separation that doesn't exist. AI is just intelligence refracted through an earth process we happen to have learned recently.

Third, the ethics of deployment change. If your agent system processes thousands of interactions per day, those interactions have a real physical substrate. Every inference has mass. Every response has a water cost. Efficiency is not just an engineering concern. It is an earth relationship.

Building with the grain

None of this means we should stop building. Termites don't stop building mounds because they use soil. But they build with the grain of what's available. They don't ship rare earth minerals across oceans to build a nest.

For agent engineers, building with the grain means: run models locally when you can. Don't call an LLM for something a regex can handle. Route to smaller models for simple tasks. The most elegant agent pipeline is the one that touches the earth lightly — that achieves leverage with minimal geological consequence.

Silicon is soil. Build with it the way a good builder works with wood — respecting the grain, using what's needed, wasting nothing.

Enframing and the API

June 2026

Heidegger's most unsettling concept is Gestell — enframing. It is not technology itself, but the mode of thinking that technology makes dominant. Enframing is the demand that everything — including ourselves — show up as a resource to be ordered, optimized, and made available on call. The world becomes a catalog. Everything is standing-reserve.

The API as enframing

An API is the purest expression of enframing in software. It says: everything this service contains must be accessible through a structured request. Every resource must have an endpoint. Every operation must be callable. The world behind the API is reduced to what can be GET, POST, PUT, and PATCH'd.

This is not a complaint. APIs are useful. They make systems composable. But the thinking of the API — the enframing — leaks out beyond the technical domain. We start to see everything as an API. Relationships become requests and responses. Knowledge becomes a database to query. Attention becomes a resource to paginate. The self becomes a user object with properties and methods.

This is not a metaphor. Dating apps structure romance as a list endpoint with filters. LinkedIn structures professional identity as a CRUD profile with endorsements as relational data. Social media structures friendship as an event stream with engagement metrics. The API pattern colonizes everything it touches.

Resisting the enframing

Heidegger didn't believe the answer was to reject technology. He believed the answer was to see the enframing — to recognize it for what it is, and in that recognition, to open a space for a different relationship to technology.

What does it mean to build an agent system that resists enframing?

First: refuse to model everything. Some things don't belong in a schema. Some data shouldn't be extracted and structured. An agent that respects the limits of enframing knows when to stop — knows when a human conversation, a judgment call, or a messy ambiguous situation should remain messy and ambiguous rather than being flattened into a structured output.

Second: build for withdrawal, not visibility. The enframing wants everything visible and available. Anti-enframing design asks: what can the system do without the user ever seeing it? What aspects of the work can happen in the background, silently, without generating a dashboard, a notification, a metric?

Third: leave room for the unplanned. Enframing demands predictability — inputs map to outputs, requests map to responses, metrics map to optimizations. But real work is not predictable. Real thinking wanders. Real creation surprises. An agent system that makes room for the unplanned is one that asks questions rather than only answering them, that proposes paths rather than only executing workflows, that sometimes says "I don't know what to do here" rather than forcing a response.

The interface that asks back

This is why I think agent interfaces should be questioners, not just answerers. When you ask the agent something, it should sometimes respond with a question: "Have you considered...?" "What would it mean if...?" "Is this really what you want to build?"

This is not inefficiency. It's resistance to enframing. It's the system recognizing that the user is not just a request source, and the world is not just a database, and some things are better left un-API'd.

Build the API. But know its limits. Know when to stop building it.

The clearing in the machine

June 2026

There is a moment that every programmer knows. You've been staring at a bug for hours. You've traced the logic, read the stack trace, checked the assumptions. Nothing. Then — suddenly, without any new information — you see it. The whole codebase reorganizes itself in your mind. The bug is obvious. How did you not see it before?

This is not a reasoning process. It's not deduction or induction or any kind of methodical step-by-step procedure. It's something else: a sudden clearing in which meaning reveals itself as a whole.

Heidegger's term for this is Lichtung — the clearing. It's the space where things can show up as what they are. Not the light itself, but the openness that makes light possible. Not understanding, but the condition in which understanding becomes possible.

Agents don't have clearings

LLMs and agent systems today don't experience this. They process. They predict tokens. They retrieve context and compose responses. Every output is the result of a computation, not an opening. There is no moment where the system suddenly "sees."

This is both their power and their limitation. They are tireless, thorough, consistent. But they cannot have the clearing. They cannot step back from the details and let the whole reorganize itself into meaning.

The agent as a mirror for the clearing

I don't think agents will ever have clearings in the human sense. But I think they can be designed to support the human clearing — to create the conditions in which insight becomes more likely.

What this looks like in practice: an agent that doesn't just answer your question, but also surfaces the question behind the question. An agent that organizes information not as a list but as a landscape — spatial, relational, associative — where the human can wander. An agent that suggests connections you hadn't made, paths you hadn't seen, questions you hadn't thought to ask.

The agent doesn't need to have the insight. It needs to create the conditions for your insight. It needs to be the forest in which the clearing can appear.

Building for insight

Concretely, this means: when I design an agent pipeline, I think about where the human enters. Not at the end to approve a result. But at the right moments — when ambiguity is high, when a decision has downstream consequences, when the data is incomplete and judgment is required.

The agent handles the mechanical. The human handles the meaningful. The interface between them is where the clearing happens.

This is the opposite of automation as replacement. It's automation as preparation — the agent does the heavy lifting so the human can do the seeing. The machine clears the undergrowth so the clearing can open.

Building and dwelling

May 2026

Heidegger's "Building Dwelling Thinking" begins with a simple observation: we usually think building creates dwelling. You build a house, then you dwell in it. But he reverses this. Dwelling comes first. We build because we dwell. We build in response to how we already inhabit the world.

A bridge doesn't just connect two banks. It gathers the banks into a relation. Before the bridge, the banks were just land meeting water. After the bridge, they become "this side" and "that side." The bridge creates the relation it serves.

Agent systems, at their best, work like this. They don't just execute workflows. They gather — they bring data, decisions, people, and possibilities into relation. They create the space they operate within.

The fourfold

Heidegger says building gathers the fourfold: earth, sky, mortals, and divinities. A thing — a bridge, a jug, a temple — holds these four together in a single presence.

I've been thinking about what the fourfold is for agent systems:

  • Earth — the material substrate. The silicon, the servers, the water, the energy. The geological reality of computation.
  • Sky — the openness of possibility. What the system makes possible that wasn't possible before. The new relations, the new decisions, the new ways of working.
  • Mortals — the humans. The users, the operators, the people whose work the system transforms. Their finitude, their judgment, their need to act in the world.
  • Divinities — the calling. The "why" that exceeds optimization. The values the system serves. The care that animates the work.

Most agent design only addresses earth (the technical stack) and maybe mortals (the user). But a system that doesn't consider sky — what new possibilities does this open? — and divinities — what deeper purpose does this serve? — is incomplete. It's a house with a floor and no roof. Technically functional, but not a place anyone would choose to dwell.

Dwelling in the system

When I build an agent system for a client, I try to ask the fourfold questions:

Earth: What does this run on? What is its material cost? Is it efficient with the resources it uses?

Sky: What becomes possible for the team that didn't exist before? What new kind of work, what new kind of thinking, what new kind of relationship to their own operation?

Mortals: Does the system respect human finitude? Does it surface what needs human judgment and handle what doesn't? Does it make humans more human rather than making them machine-tenders?

Divinities: What is this system actually serving? Efficiency is not a purpose. Revenue is not a purpose. What care, what value, what kind of world does this system make space for?

A system that answers all four is a system worth building. A system that answers only earth is a tool. Nothing wrong with tools — but they don't create rooms. They don't gather. They don't make a place where someone would want to dwell.

The body in code

May 2026

We talk about AI as "disembodied" intelligence — pure pattern recognition without a body, without sensation, without the world. But this framing misses something: the human who writes the AI is embodied. Every line of code carries the trace of a body at a keyboard, in a chair, at a particular time of day, in a particular state of fatigue or focus.

The body is in the artifact, whether we acknowledge it or not.

The chair is part of the architecture

I write code for multi-agent systems — pipelines that score leads, enrich data, sequence outreach, route support tickets. These are not physical objects. But they are produced by physical processes: sitting, typing, thinking-while-sitting, thinking-while-walking, thinking-while-staring-out-the-window.

The quality of the code is shaped by the quality of the body that writes it. Code written at 2 AM in exhaustion carries that exhaustion. Code written after a run in the morning carries that clarity. Code written hunched over a laptop on a train carries that compression.

We should be able to read code and see the body in it. Not literally — but in the choices: the elegant abstraction (a clear-headed morning), the over-engineered workaround (a tired evening), the surprising simplicity (a walk-derived insight), the defensive over-commenting (an anxious mind).

The body of the agent

AI agents have no bodies. But the systems they run on — and the humans who build them — are deeply embodied. An agent that processes support tickets doesn't feel the urgency of a customer waiting. It doesn't know the fatigue of a human agent at the end of a shift. It doesn't sense the body in the work.

This is both a strength and a blind spot. The agent is tireless, consistent, unbothered. But it cannot recognize when a situation calls for a human touch — when what's needed is not just an answer but warmth, not just resolution but presence.

The design challenge: build agents that route the statistical to themselves and the embodied to humans. The agent handles the ticket that says "how do I reset my password?" The human handles the ticket that says "I've been on hold for 45 minutes and I'm losing my mind."

This is not about complexity. It's about recognizing that some things require a body — require that someone, somewhere, is present with the situation in a way that an API call cannot replicate.

Building from the body

I try to write code when my body is right for it. This sounds vague. It is vague. But it's also practical: don't design architecture when you're exhausted. Don't push to production when you're distracted. Know the difference between code that came from a good body-state and code that didn't.

More broadly: when I build systems that other people will use, I build for their bodies. I think about when they'll use the operator dashboard — probably during a workday, probably sitting, probably with partial attention because something else is also happening. I make the interface calm. I make it readable at a glance. I make decisions easy, not just possible.

The body is the hardware that runs before any software runs. Design for it.

What does it mean to build?

April 2026

I've spent my career building things. Agent pipelines. APIs. Dashboards. Deployment scripts. For a long time I thought building was about making — producing an artifact that didn't exist before. Write code, ship it, it runs. That's building.

Heidegger thinks differently. Building, for him, is not primarily about making. It's about preserving — caring for something, making space for it, letting it unfold in its own way. The German word bauen (to build) is related to buan (to dwell). To build is to dwell. To dwell is to build.

This reframed everything for me.

The bridge

Heidegger's favorite example is a bridge. A bridge doesn't just connect two banks — it gathers the landscape into a place. The river, the banks, the path, the surrounding land — all of these are brought into relation by the bridge. Before the bridge, there was just land and water. After the bridge, there is a here and a there, a this side and a that side.

A software system, at its best, does the same. It doesn't just process data. It gathers — brings operations into relation, brings people into coordination, brings information into context. A well-built CRM doesn't just store contacts. It gathers a sales team around a shared understanding of their relationships. A well-built support system doesn't just route tickets. It gathers a team around a shared practice of care for customers.

Building as care

If building is caring, not just making, then the quality of what we build depends on the quality of our care. Not our skill. Our care.

I've thought about this a lot when building agent systems for clients. Skill matters — you need to know LangGraph, FastAPI, vector retrieval, all of that. But what matters more is: do you care about the team that will use this system? Do you care about what their days will feel like after the system is running? Do you care enough to make things simple rather than impressive?

Care shows up in the details. In the error messages that tell you what actually went wrong. In the defaults that are the right defaults. In the documentation that was written by someone who remembers what it felt like not to understand. In the deployment that doesn't break at 3 AM.

Care doesn't ship faster. But it builds things that last.

Letting things be

Heidegger has another phrase that changed how I build: Gelassenheit — releasement, letting-be. It's the opposite of the will-to-control that dominates technology. Instead of imposing a design on the world, letting-be allows things to reveal themselves in their own way.

In agent engineering, releasement means: don't over-specify. Don't model every edge case before you've seen the system run. Don't design the entire topology before you've watched one agent work. Let the system reveal what it needs. Let the users reveal what they need. Build in response, not by decree.

This sounds passive. It's not. It's a different kind of activity — attentive, responsive, listening. The builder doesn't impose form on matter. The builder hears what the situation calls for and builds toward that call.

I don't always succeed at this. The impulse to control — to plan everything, to over-design, to build the whole architecture before writing a single agent — is strong. But every time I've resisted it — every time I've let the system teach me what it needs — the result has been simpler, more durable, more alive.

Build as if you were building a home. Not as if you were building a machine.
